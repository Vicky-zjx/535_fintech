Investment Thesis

I believe that selling short-dated cash-secured puts on BAC, KO, PFE and F when their trends are positive and volatility is moderate can provide a better balance between option premium and downside risk than selling puts every week without market filters.

Investors buy puts to protect against losses they may not be able to absorb. I receive a premium for taking the other side of that risk. However, a higher premium does not necessarily mean a better opportunity: it can reflect a much greater chance or size of loss. I need to consider what I might pay out, not just what I collect.

I want to test whether the existing filters help me avoid some of those riskier periods. Selling fewer puts could be worthwhile if the losses avoided exceed the premium I give up. The opposite is also possible: calmer markets may offer so little premium that waiting reduces returns. Cboe's index put-writing research motivates this insurance argument, but it is not evidence that my four-stock strategy has an edge.

H1: The filters identify periods with lower subsequent stock downside risk.
H2: That reduction is large enough to compensate for the option premium and trading opportunities given up. My stock-price tracker tests H1, not H2. Neither claim is assumed to be true.

I expect to research this thesis over the next 6–12 months and review the evidence monthly. That is a planning horizon, not a proven period of profitability. An option is intended to last about 7 calendar days, within the unchanged 3–10 DTE range. Calculation and excluded-opportunity break-even

Let q be eligible weeks divided by all mature weeks, and let BU and BF be the existing unfiltered and filtered mean expiry liability plus cost per trade. With premium p: RU = p − BU; RF = q(p − BF); advantage = BU − qBF − (1 − q)p. The excluded opportunities' after-cost break-even premium is (BU − qBF) / (1 − q). The before-cost value uses liabilities only. This is the crossover of the two scenario returns, not an estimate of actual alpha. If no opportunities are excluded, that break-even value is undefined.

The four-stock average uses a fixed 25% weight on each normalized series. Its excluded-opportunity break-even divides average avoided liability and cost by the average excluded-week share; it is not the unweighted mean of stock-level break-even premiums. No new statistical model or confidence interval is fitted.

Final Thesis Conclusion

The historical downside-risk evidence does not yet sufficiently support my original thesis. The five-year estimates are uncertain and do not show a consistent benefit across BAC, KO, PFE and F. BAC's sensitivity to the sampling method further limits confidence. These results do not prove that filtering can never help; they do show why a favorable average alone is not enough.

The hypothetical put payoff analysis brings the question closer to its economic purpose: how much premium would cover the liability I take on? It does not show consistent reductions in that liability. Fewer eligible weeks also mean less time collecting premium. A lower weekly liability caused by taking less exposure is not, by itself, evidence of better capital use.

Actual option returns remain a separate, unresolved question. Break-even premium is a cost-and-liability hurdle, not estimated alpha. Without comparable historical candidate sets, dated quotes and premiums for selected and rejected opportunities, I cannot tell whether avoided losses exceed forgone premium, costs and the return on idle cash. The small set of real option illustrations does not establish strategy performance.

Future monitoring will compare new, matured observations with the frozen historical baseline rather than tune the filters to these results. Stock-risk monitoring is not a substitute for filled-trade and portfolio records. The existing rules remain unchanged; any claim about future strategy performance must wait for that evidence.

Macro Environment

My reasoning starts with the value of protection: an investor may pay to reduce uncertainty or avoid having to sell stock during a loss. In an orderly, gradually rising market, that demand might persist while short-horizon downside is lower. This is the situation in which I would expect the thesis to be most plausible. But smaller premiums could offset the risk improvement. Selling less insurance also means collecting less premium.

In a falling, high-volatility market, a larger premium may simply pay for a larger expected loss. The filters may keep me out of some new trades, but they do not protect stock already acquired through assignment. A cash-secured put can still suffer a substantial loss. Moving averages look backward, and historical volatility may miss the next shock: a stock can pass every filter and gap down, including around earnings because the strategy has no earnings blackout.

Interest rates affect the return I can earn on idle cash and therefore the hurdle for selling a put. They can also affect credit, demand and valuations. A recession or credit shock could pressure BAC through lending and credit conditions and F through consumer demand and financing costs. KO and PFE may respond differently, but neither is immune to a broad selloff. These are possible channels, not explanations proven by my charts or a description of current policy. The New York Fed's policy implementation overview provides background on the interest-rate channel.

A technology selloff could spread through general risk aversion even though these four stocks are not a technology portfolio. Correlations can rise during stress, so different industries do not guarantee diversification. The 30% per-name and 70% total limits bound the amount committed; they do not establish that the risks are independent. I would review the thesis under these scenarios, rather than assume its past average remains valid.

Theorem 1.1: formula, assumptions and application

Theorem 1.1: classroom connection

Source qualification: the original classroom slide has not been located. The formula below is the standard KL Chernoff–Hoeffding form, using the requested n, X̄, μ, t, a and b; the normalized symbols μ̃ and t̃ are defined here. It is not claimed to reproduce the professor's exact notation. See Hoeffding (1963), Theorem 1 and the rescaling discussion.

Let X₁, …, Xₙ be independent random variables bounded by a ≤ Xᵢ ≤ b, with a < b. Here n is the number of observations, X̄ = (X₁ + … + Xₙ)/n is their sample mean, and μ = E[X̄] is the unknown true mean (the average mean if they differ). The error tolerance is t. Define normalized μ̃ = (μ − a)/(b − a) and t̃ = t/(b − a). For a < μ < b and 0 ≤ t < b − μ:

Pr(X̄ − μ ≥ t) ≤ exp[−n D(μ̃ + t̃ ∥ μ̃)]

= [(μ̃ / (μ̃ + t̃))μ̃ + t̃ ((1 − μ̃) / (1 − μ̃ − t̃))1 − μ̃ − t̃]n

D(q ∥ p) = q ln(q/p) + (1 − q) ln((1 − q)/(1 − p)).

Endpoint terms use continuous limits; ln is the natural logarithm. My X is 1 when a completed path reaches a closing-price decline of at least 3%, and 0 otherwise. Thus a = 0, b = 1, X̄ is the observed decline frequency and μ is the unknown decline probability for the selected stock and group. A filtered observation passes the existing technical rules; unfiltered includes all valid signals, including those that pass.

The displayed theorem is an upper-tail statement. Evidence of a surprisingly low decline frequency requires the other tail: applying it to 1 − X gives Pr(X̄ ≤ μ − t) ≤ exp[−n D(μ − t ∥ μ)], for 0 ≤ t < μ. I never substitute X̄ for a known μ. The controls below use a hypothetical μ, not an estimate declared to be true.

Non-overlapping observations and joint block bootstrap

I leave the daily tracker unchanged. For this extension, specified on November 24, 2026, I fix one signal every seven XNYS sessions, anchored to November 24, 2023. Signal t through outcome t+6 cannot share a price date with the next selected path. Missing paths are excluded without shifting this schedule. Earlier prices still enter overlapping technical-indicator lookbacks, and market regimes can persist: non-overlapping outcomes do not establish independence.

I jointly resample contiguous date blocks for BAC, KO, PFE and F, carrying each signal's eligibility and already-observed outcome together. The same dates are used for every stock and both groups, so filtered remains a subset of unfiltered. I do not construct artificial price paths or pool four stocks into independent trials. This follows the blockwise approach for dependent observations discussed in Künsch's bootstrap research.

There are 5,000 draws per design and block length, with fixed PCG64 seeds. Daily blocks span 7, 14 or 28 trading dates; non-overlapping blocks contain 1, 2 or 4 selected slots, spaced seven sessions apart. Primary lengths are 14 dates and 2 slots, chosen before these calculations; the 1-slot case is an independence sensitivity, not the preferred dependence model. Blocks do not wrap from the end of history to its beginning. Every matured calendar slot is retained, including missing observations with zero weight, not zero loss. Recent unfinished paths are not resampled.

Differences below are unfiltered minus filtered; positive values favor lower risk after filtering. A frequency difference is not the same as a difference in mean maximum closing loss. Intervals require at least 20 pass and 20 fail paths for a difference, at least 20 observations for a group's frequency, and at least 95% finite bootstrap draws. Frequency-difference intervals are withheld if either pass or fail has no observed variation; degenerate resampling intervals are also withheld. Fewer than five events or non-events in either cohort triggers a sparse-event caution. The threshold of 20 is a reporting floor, not proof of adequate power.

Hypothetical put timing, expiry loss and assumed costs

I fix one opportunity on the last trading session of each week, usually Friday, using only the previous session's close and filter decision. Hypothetical expiry is the last trading session on or before the next Friday. Holiday shifts give 6–8 calendar days here, within the strategy's 3–10 DTE range. This avoids selecting a favorable weekday, but is not a replay of the live any-eligible-day, once-per-week order engine. The previous close sets a known hypothetical strike; it is not an assumed execution price.

Loss is max(strike − expiry stock close, 0), divided by strike collateral. Break-even adds the existing research budget of $3.30 per 100-share scenario; it does not change trading-engine fees. Early exercise, dividends, assignment-stock holding, cash yield, real spreads and market premiums are excluded.

Dates, data limitations, refresh architecture and reproduction

The design was registered on November 23, 2026. Signals through that frozen cutoff are retrospective; prospective signals must be strictly later, and their outcomes must have matured by the current market date. The frozen historical baseline uses only outcomes completed by November 23. This date convention is shared by the results and forward monitor. It changes no prices, trading filters or historical full-sample estimates.

I use the XNYS calendar, signal t, reference t+1 and outcome t+6. All six path closes must exist, and an outcome only enters a rolling point after it has matured. Missing prices are not interpolated, carried forward or set to zero. Invalid signals are excluded, not called filter failures. These are as-retrieved LSEG closes, not vintage data or dividend-inclusive total returns. Within-sample forward outcomes are used for evaluation, never to set the original signal.

Aggregate lines give each stock's cohort mean equal weight and require all four to meet the minimum counts. This avoids changing ticker weights, but not differences in regimes or sector risk. The unfiltered sample contains the pass and fail groups; they are not independent experiments. Five-session paths overlap, and the rolling windows overlap further. Stability counts are descriptive dates, not independent trials or confidence levels. Selecting four surviving stocks also introduces survivorship and universe-selection limitations.
